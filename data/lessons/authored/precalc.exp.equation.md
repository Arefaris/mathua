# Solving exponential equations

## Equal bases

When both sides of an equation can be written as powers of the same base, the exponents must be equal:

\[
a^m = a^n \quad \text{implies} \quad m = n.
\]

So \(2^x = 32\) becomes \(2^x = 2^5\) and \(x = 5\). The step that makes it work is rewriting the constant as a power of the base, which is why the small powers are worth recognising rather than computing.

## Logarithms as the general tool

When the two sides do not share a base, take a logarithm: \(a^x = c\) gives \(x = \log_a c\). The logarithm is the inverse of the exponential, which is what lets it pull the exponent down to where it can be solved for.

Applying the same logarithm to both sides is legitimate because a logarithm is one-to-one, so the step neither introduces nor destroys a solution.

## Combining the rules

An equation such as \(2^{x+1} = 8^x\) is solved by writing both sides with base 2, which turns it into a linear equation. Solving an exponential equation usually means either reducing both sides to equal bases or isolating one exponential and taking a logarithm.
