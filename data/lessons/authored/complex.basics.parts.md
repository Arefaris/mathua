# Real and imaginary parts

Every complex number is written \(z = a + bi\) with real \(a\) and \(b\), and the two coordinates are read off separately: \(a\) is the **real part** and \(b\) the **imaginary part**. The number sits at the point \((a, b)\) of the plane, with \(a\) measured along the real axis and \(b\) along the imaginary axis.

## What the parts mean

The parts are projections, not a decomposition into "real plus fake". Adding complex numbers adds the parts separately, and multiplying by a real number scales both, which is exactly what addition and scaling do to points in the plane.

The imaginary part is a real number: the imaginary part of \(3 - 4i\) is \(-4\), not \(-4i\). The \(i\) is the unit, and the coefficient carries the sign.

## Equality of complex numbers

Two complex numbers are equal exactly when their real parts agree and their imaginary parts agree: \(a + bi = c + di\) forces \(a = c\) and \(b = d\). That is one equation standing for two, which is what makes it useful.

So \(x + 2i = 5 + yi\) gives \(x = 5\) and \(y = 2\) immediately, and the sum \(x + y = 7\) follows. Systems of this kind are solved by matching components, never by comparing the numbers as wholes.

## Reading coefficients

The same reading works when the parts are expressions. If \(x + 3i = 5 + yi\), then the real parts give \(x = 5\) and the imaginary parts give \(y = 3\). If the left side is \(2x + 3yi\), the real part is \(2x\), so \(2x = 5\) and \(x = 5/2\).

What it does not do is compare \(i\) with numbers: \(3i = 3\) is false, because the imaginary part of the left side is three and that of the right side is zero.
