# Isosceles triangles

## The equal sides and angles

An isosceles triangle has two sides of equal length, and the angles opposite those sides are equal. The third angle, between the equal sides, is the vertex angle, and the two equal ones are the base angles.

So knowing one angle determines the others: a vertex angle of 40 leaves 70 for each base angle, and a base angle of 50 leaves 80 for the vertex.

## The symmetry

The line from the vertex angle to the midpoint of the base is an axis of symmetry: it bisects the vertex angle, meets the base at a right angle, and is the perpendicular bisector of the base. Folding the triangle along it maps one half onto the other.

That axis is the reason the base angles are equal in the first place, and it is a line worth drawing in any isosceles problem.

## Working backwards

If two angles of a triangle are equal then the opposite sides are equal, so the triangle is isosceles. The converse turns an angle observation into a length result, which is often easier than measuring.

Together the two directions mean "isosceles" can be recognised from either the sides or the angles, and the equilibrium of the two is what makes the triangle the standard example of a symmetry argument.
