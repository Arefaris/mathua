# Area from two sides and the angle between them

## The height you were not given

The area of a triangle needs a base and a height, and two sides with an angle between them supply
neither directly. Dropping a perpendicular from the far end of one side makes the height visible:
\(h = b\sin C\), which is the second side projected onto the first.

Substituting that into \(A = \tfrac{1}{2}ah\) gives \(A = \tfrac{1}{2}ab\sin C\). The angle is enough
to stand in for the height.

## Why the sine is the factor

The sine of an angle is the ratio of the opposite side to the hypotenuse in a right triangle, so
\(b\sin C\) is the part of \(b\) perpendicular to \(a\). A right angle would make that factor 1 and
give the familiar half of the product; a small angle would make it small, and the triangle a sliver
with two long sides nearly parallel.

An obtuse angle still works. Its sine is positive and equal to the sine of the supplement, so a
triangle with an included angle of \(150^{\circ}\) has the same area as one with \(30^{\circ}\) and
the same two sides.

## Using the formula

With sides 12 and 7 and an included angle of \(30^{\circ}\), the area is
\(\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 12 \cdot 7 \cdot 1/2 = 21\). No third side and no other angle is needed, which
is what makes the formula the fastest route when a triangle is described that way.
