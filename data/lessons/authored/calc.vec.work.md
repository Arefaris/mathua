# Work as a line integral

## Force along a path

The work a field does on an object moving along a path is the line integral of the field along it:

\[W = \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}.\]

Only the component of the force along the direction of travel contributes, which is why the integrand is a dot product rather than a magnitude.

## A constant force

For a constant force \((3, 4)\) moving an object from the origin to \((2, 5)\), the work is \(3 \times 2 + 4 \times 5 = 26\). Along a straight displacement the integral of a constant field is just the dot product of the force with that displacement.

For a force that varies, the integral has to be evaluated, and the path matters: the same start and end points can give different work when the field is not a gradient.

## Conservative forces

When the field is a gradient the work depends only on the endpoints, which is the content of the potential. Gravity and electrostatic force are of that kind, and the work around a closed loop is then zero.

That is also why a potential is worth finding: the work it reports is the same along every path, so the path can be chosen freely and the calculation reduced to two evaluations.
