# Quadrants

## Four quarter-turns

The coordinate axes cut the plane into four regions, and an angle's terminal side always lands in
one of them. Counting counterclockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis, the first quadrant runs from
\(0^{\circ}\) to \(90^{\circ}\), the second from \(90^{\circ}\) to \(180^{\circ}\), the third from
\(180^{\circ}\) to \(270^{\circ}\) and the fourth from \(270^{\circ}\) back to \(360^{\circ}\).

The boundaries themselves belong to no quadrant. An angle of \(90^{\circ}\) points straight up the
positive \(y\)-axis, which sits between the first and second quadrants rather than inside either.

## Reading an angle's quadrant

The quadrant is decided by how many boundaries of \(90^{\circ}\) the angle has passed. An angle
between \(180^{\circ}\) and \(270^{\circ}\) has passed two boundaries and not yet the third, so it
lies in the third quadrant.

That count is also a first check on any later work. The signs of the sine and cosine are fixed by
the quadrant, so knowing the quadrant is knowing which signs an answer can take.

## Angles beyond the first turn

An angle larger than \(360^{\circ}\) still has one terminal side, so it still has one quadrant. The
quadrant is read from the reduced angle, the same coterminal partner in the first turn. Rotating
twice and stopping at \(405^{\circ}\) is the same direction as stopping at \(45^{\circ}\), and the
quadrant follows the direction rather than the number of turns.
