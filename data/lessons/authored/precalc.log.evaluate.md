# Evaluating logarithms

## The definition

\(\log_a b\) is the exponent that turns \(a\) into \(b\): it is the \(x\) with \(a^x = b\). So \(\log_2 8 = 3\) because \(2^3 = 8\), and the definition is the whole method for exact values.

Reading a logarithm as "what power" is what keeps the arithmetic manageable. Treating it as a function to be computed invites a calculator where recognition would do.

## The rules

The three rules follow from the exponent rules: a product becomes a sum, a quotient a difference, and a power pulls its exponent out as a factor.

\[
\log_a(xy) = \log_a x + \log_a y, \qquad \log_a\frac{x}{y} = \log_a x - \log_a y, \qquad \log_a x^k = k\log_a x.
\]

The bases must match for any of them to apply, which is why converting to a common base is often the first step of a longer calculation.

## Common values

\(\log_a 1 = 0\) for every base, \(\log_a a = 1\), and \(\log_a a^k = k\). Those three cover most exact evaluations and are worth recognising rather than deriving each time.

A logarithm of a non-positive number is undefined over the reals, which is why the domain of a logarithmic function is restricted rather than an afterthought.
