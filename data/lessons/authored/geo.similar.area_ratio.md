# Areas of similar figures

## Areas scale by the square

When two figures are similar with a length ratio of \(k\), their areas are in the ratio \(k^2\). A triangle similar to another with sides three times as long has nine times the area.

The reason is that area is built from two lengths multiplied, so the ratio is applied once per dimension.

## Volumes scale by the cube

The same argument for a solid gives the cube: a model at one tenth scale has one thousandth of the volume. Combined with the square rule, a solid that is ten times longer has one hundred times the surface area and a thousand times the volume, which is why large objects lose heat slowly and small ones lose it quickly.

## Checking a claim

The rules are a cheap way to test whether an answer is plausible. If two similar figures differ in length by a factor of 2, an area answer of 4 is plausible and one of 2 or 8 is not, and the check needs no arithmetic beyond squaring.

That is often faster than redoing the calculation, and it catches the common mistake of applying the length ratio to an area.
