# Volumes of similar solids

## Volumes scale by the cube

Two similar solids with a length ratio of \(k\) have volumes in the ratio \(k^3\). A model at half scale holds one eighth of the volume, and one at one tenth scale holds one thousandth.

The cube appears because volume is built from three lengths multiplied, so the ratio is applied once per dimension.

## Area and volume together

The same solid pair has surface areas in the ratio \(k^2\) and volumes in the ratio \(k^3\). So a solid twice as long has four times the surface and eight times the volume, which is why surface-to-volume ratio falls as size grows.

That falling ratio is why large animals overheat slowly and small ones quickly, and why a powder dissolves faster than a single lump of the same mass.

## Checking a claim

The rules make a cheap plausibility check. With a length ratio of 3, a volume answer of 27 is plausible and one of 9 or 3 is not, because those are the square and the ratio itself.

Confusing the three powers is the common mistake, so the check is usually worth the second it takes.
