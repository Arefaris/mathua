# Simplifying rational expressions

A rational expression is a quotient of polynomials. Cancelling is allowed only for a factor common to the whole numerator and the whole denominator, and the cancellation changes the domain.

## Factor before cancelling

Factor the numerator and the denominator completely. A term that appears in a sum cannot be cancelled: in \(\frac{x^2+5x+6}{x+2}\), the \(x^2\) does not cancel the \(x\).

Once both are products,

\[
\frac{(x+2)(x+3)}{x+2},
\]

the common factor is visible. The factor must match exactly, including its sign; \(x+2\) and \(x-2\) are different factors.

## Cancel a common factor

Cancelling \((x+2)\) leaves \(x+3\). This is an identity wherever the original expression is defined, not an approximation. The cancelled factor is gone from the written answer, but its zero is still a hole in the original expression.

Simplification therefore does not enlarge the domain. The simplified expression is the same function on the original domain, not on all real numbers.

## Keep the domain restriction

The denominator of the original expression cannot be zero. If the cancelled factor was \(x+2\), the original expression required \(x\ne -2\), and the simplified answer still carries that restriction.

A complete explanation states it: the cancellation is valid for \(x\ne -2\). Dropping the restriction is the one error that makes a correct-looking simplification mathematically false at a single point.
