# The double-angle formulas

## Two angles that are the same

Writing \(2\theta\) does not introduce a new angle. It asks for the sine or cosine at an angle that
happens to be twice one you have already described, and the double-angle formulas answer it from
the single-angle ratios alone.

So the input is a ratio at \(\theta\) and the output is a ratio at \(2\theta\), with no need to know
\(\theta\) itself.

## Sine and cosine of twice an angle

The two formulas come from adding \(\theta\) to itself in the sum formulas. For sine the result is
\(\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta\), and for cosine it is
\(\cos(2\theta) = \cos^{2}\theta - \sin^{2}\theta\).

Both are written entirely in terms of the single-angle ratios. Given \(\sin\theta = 3/5\) on a
right triangle, the adjacent side is 4, so \(\cos\theta = 4/5\) and
\(\sin(2\theta) = 2 \cdot 3/5 \cdot 4/5 = 24/25\).

## Signs after doubling

The acute angle in the input does not make the output positive. Doubling can carry \(2\theta\) past
\(90^{\circ}\), where the cosine turns negative, and the formula reports that sign because it
subtracts one square from the other.

That is a common trap when the triangle is drawn for the original angle. The picture is a real
triangle, but \(2\theta\) is not an angle of it, and the signs come from the formula rather than
from the triangle.
