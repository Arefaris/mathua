# The multiplicity of a zero

## What multiplicity means

When a factor \(x - a\) appears \(k\) times in a polynomial, the zero at \(a\) has multiplicity \(k\). Factoring completely shows the multiplicities directly: \(p(x) = (x-2)^3(x+1)\) has a zero of multiplicity 3 at 2 and of multiplicity 1 at -1.

The total of the multiplicities is the degree, which is the fundamental theorem of algebra counted rather than stated.

## Even and odd multiplicities

An even multiplicity makes the graph touch the axis and turn back, because the factor is positive on both sides of \(a\). An odd multiplicity makes it cross, and a multiplicity of 1 crosses at an angle while a higher odd one crosses flat.

That is the whole reason the multiplicity is worth finding: the exponent tells you the shape of the graph at the zero without plotting anything.

## Finding the multiplicity

From a factored form it is the exponent. From an unfactored polynomial the multiplicity is found by differentiating: if \(p(a) = p'(a) = \cdots = p^{(k-1)}(a) = 0\) and the \(k\)th derivative is not zero, the multiplicity is \(k\).

For \((x-2)^3(x+1)\), the first two derivatives vanish at 2 and the third does not, which agrees with the exponent.
