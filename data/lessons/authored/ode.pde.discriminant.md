# Classifying a second-order PDE

## The discriminant of a second-order equation

A second-order linear equation in two variables has coefficients on its three second derivatives, and the combination of the middle coefficient squared minus four times the product of the outer two is its discriminant. That number has the same form as the one from the quadratic formula, and it plays the same role.

The equation's type is decided by its sign and not by its size, so the value is worth computing before any solution method is chosen.

## The three types

A positive discriminant makes the equation hyperbolic, a zero one parabolic, and a negative one elliptic. Each type has its own character: hyperbolic equations have wave-like solutions that propagate, parabolic ones smooth initial data, and elliptic ones are determined by boundary values alone.

Recognising the type tells you which kind of data the problem needs and which method will work.

## Comparing with the classical equations

The wave equation has a positive discriminant, the heat equation a zero one, and Laplace's equation a negative one. The three classical equations are therefore one representative each of the three types, which is why they are the examples every course uses.

A general equation is classified by placing it beside those three, and the discriminant is how that placement is done.
