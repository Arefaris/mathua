# The sum of an arithmetic sequence

## Pairing the ends

An arithmetic sequence grows by a fixed difference each step, so its first and last terms average to the same value as its second and second-to-last, and so on. Pairing the terms from the ends gives a set of pairs all with the same sum.

That pairing is Gauss's method, and it reduces a sum of many terms to a multiplication.

## The average of the pair

Every pair has the same average, which is the average of the first and last terms. So the total is the number of terms times that average, and the formula is that sentence written in symbols.

The reason the method works is symmetry: the sequence is equally spaced, so reflecting it about its centre leaves the same list.

## Why the number of terms matters

The average of a pair is easy to compute but it is not the answer on its own: the total is the average multiplied by how many terms there are. Forgetting the count is the usual error, and it is invisible without checking the size of the result.

A quick estimate catches it: a sum of twenty terms each around ten must be near two hundred, not near ten.
