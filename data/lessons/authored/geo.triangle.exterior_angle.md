# The exterior angle theorem

## The theorem

An exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two interior angles that are not adjacent to it. Extending one side past a vertex creates the exterior angle, and the two remote interior angles are the ones at the other two vertices.

The theorem is a shortcut past the two-step route through the third angle, and it is the reason the exterior angle is always larger than either remote angle.

## Why it is true

The three interior angles sum to 180, and the exterior angle is supplementary to the interior angle at the same vertex, so it also sums with that angle to 180. Equating the two expressions leaves the exterior angle equal to the other two interior angles added.

The argument is two facts about straight angles and triangles, which is why it is worth stating separately: it comes up often enough to be worth not rederiving.

## Using it

The theorem answers questions about a triangle whose third angle is not given, and it is the fastest route when the two known angles are at the far end. Since the exterior angle is a sum, it also bounds the interior angles it is made of: each is smaller than the exterior angle, which is a useful check on an answer.

The same reasoning extends to a polygon: the exterior angles of a convex polygon sum to 360, and the triangle's theorem is the case where the exterior angle at a vertex accounts for the two it does not touch.
