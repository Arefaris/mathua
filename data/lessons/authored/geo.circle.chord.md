# Chords and the perpendicular from the centre

## The perpendicular bisects the chord

The perpendicular drawn from the centre of a circle to a chord bisects it. The two right triangles it forms share the radius as hypotenuse, the distance from the centre as one leg, and the two halves of the chord as the other, so they are congruent.

The converse is also true: the perpendicular bisector of a chord passes through the centre, which is how the centre is found from a chord and is why any two chords' perpendicular bisectors meet there.

## Using the right triangle

Half the chord, the distance from the centre and the radius form a right triangle, so Pythagoras gives any one from the other two. For a radius of 13 and a distance of 5, half the chord is \(\sqrt{169 - 25} = 12\) and the whole chord is 24.

When the offsets are a Pythagorean triple the answer is a whole number, which is why the examples are built that way.

## Equal chords and equal distances

Chords of the same length lie at the same distance from the centre, and a diameter is the longest chord because its distance is zero. Those two facts follow from the right triangle: the side opposite the radius grows as the distance shrinks, so the largest chord is the one through the centre.
