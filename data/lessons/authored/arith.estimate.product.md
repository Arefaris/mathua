# Estimating products

Rounding each factor to a friendly number turns a two-digit multiplication into a one-digit fact with zeros.

## Round each factor

Round each factor to the nearest ten. Look at the ones digit: 1 to 4 rounds down, 5 to 9 rounds up. For 38 and 52, the rounded factors are 40 and 50.

A factor that is already a multiple of ten, such as 60, stays as it is.

## Multiply the estimates

Multiply the rounded factors. For \(38 \times 52\),

\[
40 \times 50 = 2000.
\]

The zeros make the multiplication short. \(4 \times 5 = 20\), and the two factors contribute three zeros in total, so the product is 2000.

## Check the size

Compare the estimate with the original factors. Both original factors are slightly below the rounded ones, so the exact product is a little below 2000. An exact answer far outside that range signals a place-value error.

Rounding one factor up and the other down can bring the estimate close to the exact product even when each rounding error is large. The two errors partly cancel, which is why an estimate from rounded factors is often more accurate than either rounded factor alone.

For \(61 \times 47\), the rounded factors are 60 and 50, so the estimate is 3000. The exact product is close to 3000, not 300 or 30000.
