# The phase shift of a sinusoid

## Moving a wave sideways

The coefficient on \(x\) sets how fast a wave repeats and the constant inside sets where it starts:
in \(y = A\sin(Bx - C)\) the wave's shape and period come from \(B\), and the constant \(C\) slides
the whole graph along the horizontal axis.

That slide is the phase shift, and it is measured in the same units as \(x\) rather than as a length
along the graph.

## Why the shift is C over B

The shift is found by asking where the argument reaches zero, because a sine starts a period at
zero. Solving \(Bx - C = 0\) gives \(x = C/B\), so an equation written as
\(y = A\sin(B(x - C/B))\) is the same wave with the shift shown directly.

Dividing is the step that is easy to skip. A constant of \(60^{\circ}\) with a coefficient of 2 is a
shift of \(30^{\circ}\), not \(60^{\circ}\), because the coefficient compresses the axis and every
horizontal distance with it.

## Reading the sign

A negative constant shifts the wave to the right, and a positive one shifts it left, which is the
opposite of what the sign suggests on first reading. Written with a plus,
\(y = A\sin(Bx + C)\) is \(y = A\sin(B(x + C/B))\), so the argument reaches zero at a negative \(x\)
and the graph has already started before the axis.

Remembering it as "the shift solves \(Bx = C\)" removes the sign question: whatever value makes the
argument zero is where the wave begins, and that value carries its own sign.
