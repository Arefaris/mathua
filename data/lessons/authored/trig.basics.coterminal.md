# Coterminal angles

## Turning past a full circle

Angles are directions, and a direction does not care how many times you went around before you
stopped. An angle of \(30^{\circ}\) and an angle of \(390^{\circ}\) point the same way, because the
second is one full turn of \(360^{\circ}\) past the first. Two angles that share a terminal side are
called coterminal.

The formal statement is the useful one: angles are coterminal exactly when they differ by a whole
number of full turns. Any multiple of \(360^{\circ}\), positive or negative, can be added without
moving the terminal side.

## Reducing an angle to one turn

Because coterminal angles differ by whole turns, every angle has exactly one coterminal partner in
the interval from \(0^{\circ}\) up to \(360^{\circ}\). Finding it is a remainder problem: divide by
\(360^{\circ}\) and keep the remainder. An angle of \(1090^{\circ}\) leaves \(10^{\circ}\) after
three full turns, so \(10^{\circ}\) is its partner in the first turn.

This reduction is what makes a large angle workable. Questions about direction can always be asked
of the reduced angle, and the reduced angle is the one a picture or a unit circle can show.

## Negative angles

The same idea covers angles measured the other way. A negative angle sweeps clockwise, and adding
\(360^{\circ}\) repeatedly brings it into the same window. An angle of \(-90^{\circ}\) is coterminal
with \(270^{\circ}\), since \(-90 + 360 = 270\). Nothing new is needed: a negative angle is simply
one that is more than a full turn below zero.
