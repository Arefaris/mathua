# Compound interest

## The yearly factor

Interest compounded once a year multiplies a balance by \(1 + r\) each year, where \(r\) is the rate as a decimal. A rate of 25% is a factor of 1.25, and a rate of 50% is 1.5.

So the balance after \(t\) years is \(P(1+r)^t\), which is the exponential model with a base greater than 1 and the number of years as the exponent.

## Repeated multiplication

The compounding is repeated multiplication, which is why the growth accelerates: an investment of 500 at 25% is 625 after one year, 781.25 after two, and 976.5625 after three, and the yearly gain itself grows.

More frequent compounding uses the same idea with more periods of smaller size, and the limit as the periods shorten is where the number \(e\) appears.

## Why it beats simple interest

Simple interest adds the same amount every year, so the balance grows linearly: \(P(1 + rt)\). Compound interest multiplies, so the balance grows exponentially, and the difference is small at first and enormous later, which is the same distinction that separates an exponential model from a linear one.
