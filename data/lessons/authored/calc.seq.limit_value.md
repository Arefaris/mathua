# The limit of a rational sequence

## Only the highest powers matter

For a ratio of polynomials as the index grows, the highest power in each dominates everything else. Dividing numerator and denominator by that power leaves the other terms tending to zero and the limit as the ratio of the two leading coefficients.

So the limit is read off the leading terms in one step, and the lower ones never enter.

## Comparing the degrees

The method depends on the two degrees. Equal degrees give the ratio of the leading coefficients, a numerator of higher degree gives divergence to infinity, and a denominator of higher degree gives zero.

Those three cases cover every rational sequence, and separating them is the whole of the reasoning.

## When the degrees differ

A numerator of higher degree grows faster than the denominator, so the ratio increases without bound. A denominator of higher degree shrinks the ratio toward zero. Only the equal case produces a finite non-zero limit.

Recognising the case before computing is what keeps the arithmetic to one division rather than a long comparison.
