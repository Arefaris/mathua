# Distance in three dimensions

## Adding the third dimension

The distance formula extends to space by adding one more squared difference:

\[
d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2 + (z_2-z_1)^2}.
\]

The argument is Pythagoras applied twice: first within one face of the box the two points span, and then across the diagonal. There is nothing new to prove, only one more term.

## A Pythagorean quadruple

The answer is a whole number when the three differences form a Pythagorean quadruple, such as 2, 3 and 6 with distance 7. Those are the choices worth recognising, because otherwise the distance is left as a square root rather than rounded.

Rounding is the mistake to avoid: a distance of \(\sqrt{53}\) is not 7.28, it is \(\sqrt{53}\).

## The same formula everywhere

The same construction gives distance in any number of dimensions, and it is the definition of distance in the coordinate model rather than a theorem about space. A vector's length is the distance from its tail to its head, so the formula is also how a vector's magnitude is computed.

That is why the formula and the Pythagorean theorem are the same fact in two languages, and why neither is really about triangles.
