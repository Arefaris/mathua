# Distance in the complex plane

The modulus \(|z - w|\) is the distance between two complex numbers, because subtracting represents the displacement from \(w\) to \(z\), and the modulus measures its length. The complex plane then inherits every tool Euclidean geometry has.

## Differences as displacements

Subtracting complex numbers subtracts the parts, so \(z - w\) is the vector from \(w\) to \(z\). That vector does not depend on the origin: moving both points by the same amount leaves the difference unchanged.

This is why distance problems are written as moduli. The midpoint of two points is \((z + w)/2\), and the displacement from the midpoint to either point has modulus half the distance.

## The modulus of a difference

With \(z = a + bi\) and \(w = c + di\), the difference is \((a-c) + (b-d)i\) and its modulus is \(\sqrt{(a-c)^2 + (b-d)^2}\), which is the distance formula of the plane.

Between \(1 + 2i\) and \(4 + 6i\): the difference is \(3 + 4i\), and \(|3+4i| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5\). The coordinates \((3,4)\) are the legs of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is the distance.

## Recognising triples

When the coordinate differences are a Pythagorean triple, the distance is an integer and the arithmetic is exact. Triples like \((3,4,5)\), \((6,8,10)\), \((5,12,13)\) and their sign changes cover most textbook cases.

The sign changes do not matter, because the modulus squares each component. That is worth stating explicitly: \(|3 + 4i| = |3 - 4i| = |-3 - 4i| = 5\).
