# Inscribed and central angles

## The inscribed-angle theorem

An angle whose vertex lies on a circle and whose sides are chords is inscribed. The theorem says an inscribed angle is half the central angle that subtends the same arc.

An arc with a central angle of 100 degrees therefore subtends an inscribed angle of 50 degrees. The central angle must be even for the inscribed one to be a whole number of degrees, which is why the examples use such values.

## The semicircle case

When the arc is a semicircle the central angle is 180 degrees, so the inscribed angle is 90: an angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle. That special case is the most useful one, because it turns a circle question into a right-triangle question.

The converse is also true, and it is how a right angle is constructed with a compass and a straightedge.

## Angles on the same arc

Two inscribed angles that subtend the same arc are equal, since each is half the same central angle. Points on the same circular arc therefore *see* a chord at the same angle, which is the fact behind the inscribed-angle definition of a circle.

The theorem is also the reason a cyclic quadrilateral has opposite angles summing to 180: opposite angles see the two complementary arcs, and half of each pair of arcs adds to half of 360.
