# Rationalizing a denominator

A denominator containing a square root is not wrong, but it is inconvenient to compare and to add. Rationalizing multiplies the fraction by a form of one that moves the root into the numerator.

## Multiply by the root

For a denominator \(\sqrt{b}\), multiply numerator and denominator by \(\sqrt{b}\):

\[
\frac{k}{\sqrt{b}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{b}}{\sqrt{b}}=\frac{k\sqrt{b}}{b}.
\]

The value of the fraction is unchanged because the multiplier is one. The denominator is now the integer \(b\), and the only root left is in the numerator.

## Clear the denominator

Reduce the fraction \(\frac{k\sqrt{b}}{b}\). If \(k\) is a multiple of \(b\), the coefficient becomes an integer and the answer has the form \(a\sqrt{b}\).

For example, \(\frac{12}{\sqrt{3}}\) becomes \(\frac{12\sqrt{3}}{3}=4\sqrt{3}\). The coefficient is 4 and the radicand is 3. Reducing is part of rationalizing, not an optional extra.

## Choose the squarefree radicand

The radicand is written squarefree: no perfect square divides it. If a perfect square does divide the radicand, it is moved out of the root and absorbed into the coefficient.

Thus \(\sqrt{12}\) is written \(2\sqrt{3}\), not \(\sqrt{12}\). The pair \((a,b)\) means the coefficient and the squarefree radicand, in that order, which is why `4,3` and `3,4` are different answers.
