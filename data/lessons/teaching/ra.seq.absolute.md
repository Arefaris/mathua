# Absolute and Conditional Convergence

**Absolute convergence:** a series converges absolutely when the series of the absolute values of its terms converges; it converges conditionally when it converges but not absolutely.

## Absolute convergence

A series converges absolutely when the series formed from the absolute values of its terms converges. Absolute convergence implies ordinary convergence, and the proof uses the Cauchy criterion: the absolute values control the size of any block of terms, so the partial sums form a Cauchy sequence and hence converge. The comparison test and the ratio and root tests all establish absolute convergence, since they bound the absolute values. When a test gives absolute convergence there is nothing conditional about it.

## Conditional convergence

When the absolute values diverge but the signed series itself converges, the convergence is conditional. The alternating harmonic series is the standard example: the absolute values form the harmonic series, which diverges, yet the alternating signs make the partial sums converge. The alternating series test explains why. If the terms alternate in sign, decrease in size and tend to zero, their partial sums form two decreasing and increasing sequences that trap the sum between consecutive partial sums.

## Reading the tests correctly

The order of the definitions matters. A convergence test that proves absolute convergence has settled the series completely. A test such as the alternating series test proves convergence but says nothing about the absolute values, so it establishes at most conditional convergence and the absolute question must be answered separately. For example, the alternating reciprocal squares converge absolutely, while the alternating harmonic series converge only conditionally, and the two look alike until the absolute values are examined.
