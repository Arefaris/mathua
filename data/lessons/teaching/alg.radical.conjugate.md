# Rationalizing with Conjugates

**Conjugate:** the same expression with the sign between two terms reversed; multiplying an expression by its conjugate uses the difference of squares to clear square roots.

## The conjugate and the difference of squares

The conjugate of a sum of two square roots is the same sum with the middle sign reversed, and the product of a conjugate pair is a difference of squares. Since each square root squares to a whole number, the product contains no radicals at all. This is exactly the trick needed when a denominator carries a sum of roots, because multiplying the numerator and denominator by the conjugate clears the denominator.

## Rationalizing a denominator

To rationalize a denominator that is a sum or difference of square roots, multiply the numerator and the denominator by the conjugate. The denominator becomes a whole number, and the expression is rewritten in a form without a root below the line. The value is unchanged because the multiplier is a form of one, exactly as it is when rationalizing a single root.

## Working an example

For a denominator that is the square root of five plus the square root of two, the conjugate is the same difference, and the product of the pair is five minus two, or three. The whole expression is then the numerator times the conjugate, divided by three. Every step is an application of the difference of squares, and the arithmetic is entirely with whole numbers once the roots cancel.
