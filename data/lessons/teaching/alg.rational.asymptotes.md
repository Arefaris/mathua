# Asymptotes of Rational Functions

**Asymptote:** a line that the graph of a function approaches ever more closely without meeting; for a rational function, vertical asymptotes come from the zeros of the denominator and horizontal ones from the degrees.

## Vertical asymptotes

A vertical asymptote occurs where the denominator is zero and the numerator is not, because the function grows without bound as the input approaches that value. Factoring the denominator locates the candidates, and each factor that does not also cancel from the numerator gives a true asymptote. A factor that cancels instead gives a hole, a single point missing from the graph rather than a barrier.

## Horizontal asymptotes

The horizontal asymptote is decided by the degrees of the numerator and denominator. When the denominator's degree is larger the horizontal asymptote is the horizontal axis; when the two degrees are equal it is the ratio of the leading coefficients; and when the numerator's degree is larger there is no horizontal asymptote, though a slanted one may appear. These three cases cover every rational function.

## Sketching the graph

The asymptotes are the scaffolding of a rational graph: they divide the plane into regions, and the curve lives within each region without crossing its own asymptotes. Plotting a few points and the intercepts fixes the shape, and the sign of the function near each vertical asymptote shows whether the curve rises or falls toward it. Holes from cancelled factors are marked as open circles.
