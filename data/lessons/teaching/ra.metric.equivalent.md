# Equivalent Metrics

**Equivalent metrics:** two metrics on the same set that generate the same open sets, hence the same convergent sequences and continuous functions, though possibly different Cauchy sequences.

## Topological equivalence

Two metrics on the same set are topologically equivalent when they generate the same collection of open sets. Because the open sets determine the neighbourhoods, the convergent sequences, the continuous functions, the closure and the compactness, topologically equivalent metrics agree on all of these. On a finite-dimensional space many different formulas for the distance are topologically equivalent, which is why the choice between them is a matter of convenience. The notion of sameness here is topological, not metric.

## Bounded and unbounded metrics

Replacing a metric by a bounded version, such as the metric divided by one plus itself, gives a topologically equivalent metric whose distances are all below one. Boundedness is therefore not a topological property: it can be created or destroyed without changing the open sets. What changes is the notion of Cauchy sequence, since a sequence can be Cauchy for one metric and not for the other, so the bounded replacement may fail to be complete even when the original is. Completeness, unlike openness, is a metric property, not a topological one.

## Lipschitz and uniform equivalence

Stronger than topological equivalence is Lipschitz equivalence, in which each metric is bounded by a constant multiple of the other. Lipschitz equivalence preserves not only the open sets but also the Cauchy sequences and completeness, because it controls the two distances uniformly. Between it and topological equivalence sits uniform equivalence, where the identity is uniformly continuous in both directions. The sequence of progressively weaker notions measures how much structure each metric carries beyond the bare topology.
