# Inverse Hyperbolic Functions

**Inverse hyperbolic function:** the inverse of a hyperbolic function, defined by a logarithm because the hyperbolic functions are built from exponentials.

## Why a logarithm appears

The hyperbolic functions are combinations of exponentials, so solving for the input of a hyperbolic function means solving an equation in an exponential, and the answer is a logarithm. The inverse hyperbolic sine of a value is the logarithm of that value plus the square root of the value squared plus one, and the inverse hyperbolic cosine is the logarithm of the value plus the square root of the value squared minus one. The inverse hyperbolic tangent is half the logarithm of one plus the value over one minus the value.

## Domains and ranges

The inverse hyperbolic sine is defined on the whole line, because the hyperbolic sine takes every real value. The inverse hyperbolic cosine is defined only from one upward, because the hyperbolic cosine has a minimum of one there, and its range is the nonnegative half line. The inverse hyperbolic tangent is defined strictly between minus one and one, because the hyperbolic tangent takes values in that open interval. Each domain is the range of the corresponding hyperbolic function.

## Worked reading

Given a hyperbolic sine equal to a fraction from a right triangle, the hyperbolic cosine follows from the basic identity: it is the square root of one plus the square of the fraction, which for a Pythagorean triple is another exact fraction. A hyperbolic sine of three fifths gives a hyperbolic cosine of four fifths, and the inverse hyperbolic sine is then the logarithm of the sum. The computation stays exact because the identity replaces the exponential with a triangle relation.
