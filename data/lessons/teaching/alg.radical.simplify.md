# Simplifying Radical Expressions

**Simplified radical:** a square root split into a whole-number factor and a square root of the largest possible squarefree number.

## Pulling out perfect squares

A radical is simplified by factoring out the largest perfect square inside it, because the square root of a product is the product of the square roots. The whole-number part that comes out is the square root of that perfect square, and what remains under the radical is squarefree, so no further square can be removed. The result is unique once the whole-number factor is as large as possible.

## An example by hand

For a number that is a perfect square times a small squarefree factor, the simplification is immediate: write the number as the square times the squarefree part, then take the square root of the square. For instance a number that is sixteen times two becomes four times the square root of two. Repeating the process with larger squares keeps the whole-number factor growing until only a squarefree part remains.

## Radicals in algebra

The same rule applies to algebraic radicals, where a perfect square may include variables raised to even powers. A variable raised to an even power leaves the radical entirely, with half the exponent, while an odd power leaves one copy behind. Restricting variables to nonnegative values avoids the ambiguity of the sign, which is why simplifying a radical expression is cleaner than inverting a square.
