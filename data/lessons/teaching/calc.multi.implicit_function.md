# The Implicit Function Theorem

**Implicit function theorem:** an equation in two variables defines one variable as a function of the other near a point where the partial derivative in that variable is not zero, with the derivative given by a ratio.

## When the equation gives a function

An equation in two variables usually does not name one variable as a function of the other, but near a point it often does. The implicit function theorem says that if the partial derivative with respect to the dependent variable is not zero at the point, then the equation can be solved for that variable on a small neighbourhood, giving a differentiable function whose graph lies on the contour. The nonvanishing derivative is the condition that the contour crosses the point transversally rather than turning back on itself, which is exactly what makes a single-valued branch possible.

## The derivative formula

The derivative of the implicit function is minus the ratio of the partial derivative with respect to the independent variable to the partial derivative with respect to the dependent variable. The minus sign comes from differentiating the equation and solving for the slope, since the total derivative of the constant is zero. Differentiating again gives the second derivative and the curvature. The formula is the two-variable version of the one-variable implicit differentiation already used on circles and other curves.

## Consequences and uses

The theorem is what lets the level sets of a function be described as graphs, and it underlies the implicit and inverse function theorems in several variables, the tangent plane to a level surface, and the way constraints are handled in optimisation. In economics it gives the marginal rate of substitution as a ratio of partial derivatives. Its hypothesis is local: away from the point the branch may fail to exist or may split, which is why the conclusion is only about a neighbourhood.
