# Compound Interest

**Compound interest:** interest calculated on the principal and on the interest already earned, so the balance is multiplied by the same factor once per period.

## The formula and its parts

The balance after a number of periods is the principal multiplied by one plus the interest rate per period raised to the number of periods. The principal is the starting amount, the rate is the fraction earned each period, and the exponent counts the periods, so the formula is a single multiplication repeated as many times as interest is applied. Reading the rate as a fraction, not a percentage, is the step that most often trips up a first attempt.

## Compounding more often

When interest is compounded more than once a year, the annual rate is divided by the number of periods and the exponent is multiplied by the same number. More frequent compounding yields slightly more because each sub-period earns interest on interest sooner. The limit of ever more frequent compounding is continuous growth, which replaces the repeated factor by an exponential with the rate in the exponent.

## Working an example

A principal of one thousand at five percent compounded yearly for three years multiplies by the growth factor three times, giving a balance slightly above one thousand one hundred and fifty-seven. The arithmetic is one power and one multiplication, and the result is rounded to the nearer cent because money is counted in cents. Comparing the balance with the simple-interest value shows the amount the interest on interest has added.
