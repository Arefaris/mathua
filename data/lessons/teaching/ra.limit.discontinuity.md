# Classification of Discontinuities

**Discontinuity:** a point at which a function fails to be continuous; the failure is classified by whether the one-sided limits exist and whether they agree with each other and with the value.

## The three kinds

A discontinuity at a point is classified by the behaviour of the one-sided limits. It is removable when the two-sided limit exists but differs from the value, or the value is undefined; the gap can be closed by redefining a single number. It is a jump when both one-sided limits exist, are finite, and are different. It is essential, or of the second kind, when at least one one-sided limit fails to exist, whether by escaping to infinity or by oscillating without a limit.

## Removable and jump examples

The function equal to zero everywhere except at zero, where it equals one, has a removable discontinuity: the limit there is zero and the value is one, so redefining the single value repairs the function. The sign function has a jump at zero, with left limit minus one and right limit one, and no redefinition at the single point can bridge the two. The distinction matters because a removable discontinuity leaves the limit intact, while a jump is a genuine step that no change at one point can remove.

## Points at infinity and oscillation

Discontinuities are not only finite jumps. The function one over x has an infinite discontinuity at zero, since neither one-sided limit is finite. The function that oscillates faster and faster near zero, such as sine of one over x, has an essential discontinuity there because the one-sided limits do not exist at all. Monotone functions are better behaved: their only discontinuities can be jumps, and there can be no more than countably many of them.
