# Consumer Surplus

**Consumer surplus:** the area between the demand curve and the market price, the total benefit to buyers beyond what they pay.

## Reading the area

Demand says how much buyers would take at each price. At the market price the quantity sold is the point where the demand curve reaches that price. Buyers would have paid more for every unit to the left of that quantity, so the area between the demand curve and the price line up to the quantity sold measures their gain, called the consumer surplus. It is the integral of the demand curve from zero to the quantity sold, minus the rectangle of the price times that quantity.

## A linear demand example

For a demand curve that falls linearly, the surplus has a closed form. The quantity sold is the price difference divided by the slope, and the surplus is the area of a triangle, one half the base times the height. In terms of the price the surplus is one half the square of the difference between the choke price, where demand would fall to zero, divided by the slope of the demand. For a slope of one the surplus is one half the square of that price difference, a tidy arithmetic answer.

## Producer surplus and welfare

The producer surplus is the mirror area between the price line and the supply curve, the gain to sellers. Total welfare is the sum of the two surpluses. A tax or a price control changes the quantity and so changes both triangles; the lost area between the old and new quantities is the deadweight loss. All of these are integrals or triangle areas, which is why the consumer and producer surpluses are taught as applications of the definite integral.
