# Operations with Radicals

**Like radicals:** radical terms with the same number under the root; their whole-number coefficients combine exactly as like terms combine in a polynomial.

## Adding and subtracting like radicals

Radical terms behave like algebraic terms with the radical playing the part of the variable. Two terms with the same radical are like terms, so their coefficients add or subtract and the radical is carried along unchanged. Terms with different radicals cannot be combined, just as unlike variables cannot, and the sum is left as written. Recognizing the like radical, often after simplifying each term, is the whole task.

## Simplifying before combining

Often two radicals that look different become like radicals after each is simplified, because the perfect squares come out as coefficients. Simplifying first and combining after is the reliable order, since combining incompatible terms and then trying to simplify leads to unfinished answers. Once every term is in simplest form, the like radicals stand out.

## Multiplying and dividing

Multiplication and division need no matching radicals: the product rule lets the numbers under the roots multiply, and the quotient rule lets them divide, with the result simplified afterwards. Distributing a radical over a sum uses the same product rule term by term. A product of two identical radicals removes the root entirely, which is the fact behind rationalizing a denominator.
