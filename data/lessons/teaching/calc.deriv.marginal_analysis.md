# Marginal Analysis

**Marginal analysis:** the use of the derivative of a cost, revenue or profit function to read the rate of change of an economic quantity per additional unit.

## Marginals as derivatives

In economics the marginal cost is the derivative of the total cost with respect to the quantity produced, and the marginal revenue is the derivative of total revenue. These are the rates at which the totals change when one more unit is made or sold, approximated by the derivative. A firm comparing marginal cost with marginal revenue is comparing the derivative of cost with the derivative of revenue, and the profit is maximised where the two marginal rates are equal, because that is where profit stops increasing with quantity.

## Reading the cost curve

A typical cost function has a fixed term and a variable term that grows faster than linearly, so the marginal cost increases with quantity. The marginal cost at a chosen quantity is just the derivative evaluated there, a single arithmetic substitution once the cost function is differentiated. Average cost is the total divided by the quantity, and the average cost is minimised where it equals the marginal cost, a fact that follows from differentiating the quotient. Marginal and average cost therefore answer different questions about the same curve.

## Elasticity and decisions

Marginal reasoning extends to elasticity, the proportional change in demand per proportional change in price, which is built from derivatives of the demand function. A decision to produce one more unit is worthwhile when the marginal revenue exceeds the marginal cost, and the point of equality is the profit-maximising quantity. Because these are derivative conditions, they find interior optima, and the endpoints of the feasible range must be checked separately, exactly as in any optimisation problem.
