# Improper Integrals

**Improper integral:** an integral over an unbounded interval or of an unbounded integrand, defined as a limit of ordinary integrals and called convergent when that limit exists and is finite.

## Two kinds of improperness

An integral is improper when the interval is unbounded or when the integrand becomes unbounded somewhere on it. In either case the definition replaces the offending endpoint by a parameter and takes a limit: the integral from a finite point to infinity is the limit of the integrals up to a moving bound, and an integral up to a point where the integrand blows up is the limit of the integrals up to a point just short of it. The integral converges when the limit exists and is finite, and diverges otherwise, including when the limit is infinite.

## Power functions and the p-test

For the reciprocal powers the behaviour is decided entirely by the exponent. The integral from one to infinity of the exponent minus p converges exactly when p exceeds one, to the reciprocal of p minus one, and diverges for p at most one, where the antiderivative fails to tend to a finite limit. The same threshold appears for a singularity at zero, with the roles of the exponents reversed. This p-test is the reference against which other improper integrals are compared.

## Comparison and absolute convergence

An improper integral of a nonnegative integrand converges when it is bounded above by a convergent improper integral, by the monotone convergence of the partial integrals. Comparing term by term transfers convergence or divergence in either direction, exactly as for series. Absolute convergence of an improper integral implies convergence, and a bounded integrand with an unbounded interval can still converge by cancellation, as the integral of a sine over a ray shows, so the comparison tests must be applied to the absolute value when the sign is not controlled.
