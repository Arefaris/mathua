# The Completeness Axiom

**Completeness:** the axiom that every nonempty set of real numbers bounded above has a least upper bound in the reals; it is the property that separates the reals from the rationals.

## The axiom

An ordered field can satisfy the arithmetic and order axioms and still be full of gaps. The completeness axiom closes every gap at once: it asserts that any nonempty set bounded above has a supremum that is itself a real number. Together with the ordered-field axioms this pins the reals down uniquely. Completeness is not a theorem about arithmetic; it is the extra assumption that makes the real line a continuum rather than a dense collection of holes.

## Why the rationals fail

The rationals are an ordered field, but they are not complete. Consider the set of rationals whose square is less than two. It is nonempty and bounded above, because every element is less than two. If it had a rational supremum that supremum would have to square to two, and no rational number does. So the set is bounded above but has no least upper bound inside the rationals. The missing bound is the square root of two, and its absence is the gap that completeness fills.

## Equivalent formulations

Completeness can be stated in several equivalent ways. It is equivalent to the nested interval property, to the convergence of every bounded monotone sequence, to the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem, and to the statement that every Cauchy sequence of reals converges. Each formulation is a different face of the same single assumption, and any one of them may be taken as the axiom and the rest proved. This is why a proof may cite the bounded monotone theorem when it really needs completeness.
