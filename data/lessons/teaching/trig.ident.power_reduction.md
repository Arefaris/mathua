# Power-Reduction Formulas

**Power reduction:** identities that replace a squared sine or cosine by a first-power expression in the double angle, lowering the degree at the cost of doubling the angle.

## The two squares

The square of the sine of an angle is half of one minus the cosine of twice the angle, and the square of the cosine is half of one plus the cosine of twice the angle. Both come from the double-angle formula for the cosine, which expresses the cosine of twice the angle as one minus twice the square of the sine, and the two versions are solved for the squares. The tangent squared follows by dividing the sine square by the cosine square.

## Higher powers

Repeated application lowers any even power. A fourth power is the square of a square, so applying the reduction twice leaves a constant, a cosine of twice the angle, and a cosine of four times the angle. The odd powers keep one factor of the function and reduce the remaining even power. This is the standard way to prepare a power of a trigonometric function for integration, because first powers and cosines of multiple angles have elementary antiderivatives.

## Worked reading

Given the cosine of twice an angle as an exact fraction, the square of the sine is one minus that fraction, divided by two. A cosine of seven twenty-fifths therefore gives a sine square of nine twenty-fifths, so the sine itself is three fifths, with the sign decided by the quadrant of the original angle. The computation never needs a calculator, because the reduction keeps the values exact.
